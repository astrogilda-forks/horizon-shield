The contractor says material prices went up. Is the increase justified?

Toshikatsu Oga  /  September 27, 2026

Materials did go up, and the public index says by how much. The BLS Producer Price Index for construction materials (series WPUSI012011) stood at 375.908 in August 2026, against 341.458 in August 2025, a rise of 10.1% over twelve months (BLS PPI via FRED, monthly observations). That is a real reason for a materials line to be higher than last year's. It is not a reason for the total to be higher by the same percentage, because labor, permits and disposal did not move with it, and it is not a reason for any particular line to be above what the import and distribution chain adds up to. On a roofing quote where the shingle line is $4,420, a 10.1% rise is about $446. If the contractor's "materials went up" explains $2,000, something else is in there.

What the index is

The Producer Price Index measures what producers receive for their output, sampled monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The construction materials special index (WPUSI012011, base 1982 = 100) averages across lumber, steel, concrete, asphalt products, gypsum, insulation and the rest of what goes into a building. Recent values (BLS via FRED): 341.458 in August 2025, 368.959 in June 2026, 374.866 in July 2026 and 375.908 in August 2026. The twelve-month change is 375.908 / 341.458 = 1.101, or 10.1%.

Because it is an average, the index can be moving up while your particular material is flat, or the reverse. For asphalt shingles there is a more specific public record: the Census import value for HS 6807.90.0010, which is what our reports use to build the shingle floor (Census IMDB 2026-07). For lumber, copper and steel there are commodity indexes and exchange prices. The general index tells you the direction; the specific record tells you the amount.

A materials increase is not a total increase

Take the sample roofing quote used on this site: $19,872 in total, of which architectural shingles are $4,420 and underlayment, flashing and vents are $1,850 (USCCDB v1.0; see the sample report). Materials are $6,270, about 32% of the quote. If every material on it rose 10.1%, the quote would rise by about $633, or 3.2% of the total. Labor is priced from wages, which the BLS also publishes and which did not rise 10% in a year in Austin; the permit fee is on the city's schedule; the dumpster is priced by the haul. So "materials went up 10%" should move the total by roughly a third of that, and a contractor who adds 10% to everything is applying a materials index to labor.

The specific check: above the floor by how much?

For shingles, the question is not whether prices rose but where the line sits against the chain: the Census import landed value of $0.4667 per kilogram for January to July 2026 (Census IMDB), the Census wholesale gross margin of 28.35% (Census AIES 2024) and a contractor markup of 15% to 25% (TxDOT, Caltrans, FDOT), which give about $82 to $89 per square. A line at $170 per square is not explained by a 10% rise in materials, because the floor already reflects 2026 import prices; it is explained, if at all, by freight, brand and an extra tier of distribution, and the contractor can name them. The full arithmetic is in what a roof replacement should cost in Austin.

What to ask when a contractor cites rising prices

  1. Which materials, and by how much? A dated supplier quote or invoice for the product on the quote settles it in one page.
  2. Does the increase apply to the materials lines only, or has it been applied to labor and overhead as well?
  3. Is the quote valid for a stated period? Material prices move monthly, not daily; a quote that expires tonight is a sales technique, not a market fact. See the contractor says the price is only good today.
  4. If the price rose after you signed, what does the contract say about price changes? Many contracts fix the price; some allow a materials escalation clause, and that clause should name the index and the trigger.

Questions

How much have construction material prices gone up?

The BLS Producer Price Index for construction materials (WPUSI012011) was 375.908 in August 2026 against 341.458 in August 2025, a rise of 10.1% in twelve months. It is an average across materials; individual products differ.

If materials rose 10%, should my quote be 10% higher?

No. Materials are only part of a quote. On the sample roofing quote used on this site, materials are about 32% of the total, so a 10.1% rise in materials moves the total by about 3.2%. Labor, permits and disposal do not move with a materials index.

How do I check a contractor's claim that prices went up?

Ask for a dated supplier quote or invoice for the product on the quote, ask whether the increase was applied to materials only, and compare the line to the public chain for that material: for shingles, about $82 to $89 per square in the current data.

Can a contractor raise the price after I sign?

Only if the contract allows it. Read the contract for a price escalation clause; if there is one, it should name the index and the trigger. If the price is fixed, it is fixed.
